Week 1 – Introduction to AI Governance and Assurance

Overview

This week’s activities introduced the foundations of AI governance and assurance by examining real-world AI incidents, exploring established AI risk frameworks, and identifying AI systems used in everyday life. The exercises highlighted that AI governance extends beyond technical implementation and requires consideration of ethics, accountability, transparency, and the impacts that AI systems can have on individuals and society.

⸻

Task 1 – AI Incident Database

The AI Incident Database (AIID) provides a publicly accessible catalogue of documented AI-related incidents from around the world. It records verified incidents using evidence from government investigations, academic research, court proceedings, company statements, and reputable news organisations.

I searched for the incidents discussed in this week’s lecture and reviewed how each incident was documented. For each incident I recorded:

* Incident ID
* Incident title
* Summary of what occurred
* AI classification
* People affected
* Sources of evidence

One of the most interesting examples was Robodebt, which was presented as an example of automated decision-making rather than true artificial intelligence. I agree with this position because the system relied on predefined rules and income averaging rather than learning from data or adapting its behaviour. Although it was commonly described as AI, it was better characterised as an automated decision-making system.

Reviewing the “Reports” and “Alleged Causes” sections demonstrated that the AI Incident Database requires multiple credible sources before recording an incident. This provides confidence that incidents are based on documented evidence rather than opinion or speculation.

⸻

Task 2 – MIT AI Risk Repository

The MIT AI Risk Repository provides a structured taxonomy of AI risks by categorising both the type of harm that occurred and how that harm arose.

Using the incidents identified in Task 1, I matched each incident to several relevant risk domains. This demonstrated that a single AI incident often spans multiple categories including fairness, privacy, human rights, and governance.

I also classified one incident using the repository’s Causal Taxonomy by considering:

* Whether the harm resulted primarily from human decisions or the AI system itself.
* Whether the outcome was intentional or unintended.
* Whether the problem originated before or after deployment.

This exercise showed that understanding why an AI system failed is just as important as identifying what harm occurred.

Task 3 – AI in Daily Life

Reflecting on my own use of technology over the past few days highlighted how frequently AI systems are encountered during everyday activities. Examples included:

* ChatGPT for generative AI assistance.
* YouTube recommendation algorithms.
* Apple Face ID using computer vision and biometric recognition.

Classifying these systems reinforced that AI exists in many different forms and that most people interact with several AI technologies each day without necessarily recognising them.

Reflection

This week’s activities demonstrated that AI governance is not simply about developing AI systems but ensuring they operate safely, fairly, and responsibly. The Robodebt case highlighted how automated systems can cause significant harm when appropriate governance and human oversight are lacking.

The AI Incident Database and MIT AI Risk Repository both provide valuable resources for analysing AI failures from different perspectives. The incident database focuses on documenting real-world events, while the MIT repository provides a structured framework for understanding the underlying risks. Together they offer practical tools that will be useful throughout this unit.

As someone studying cybersecurity, I found strong parallels between AI governance and information security governance. Both disciplines emphasise risk management, accountability, transparency, and protecting people from unintended consequences arising from technology.
